Deep inelastic scattering on polarized lithium: the physics behind the planned measurements

2026-08-17 · revised 2026-08-26 · companion to the cos 2φ projection report, the reconstruction-chain report and plans/01 (physics case) · written for students and colleagues joining the program · Appendix A: modification history

Polarized lithium beams would provide the EIC with spin-polarized nuclei light enough for ab initio computation yet complex enough to exhibit genuine nuclear structure. ⁶Li is a spin-1 nucleus well described as a deuteron bound to an inert α core; ⁷Li is its spin-3/2 partner, an α plus a triton. Starting from the DIS formalism, this note develops the four measurements the program is built around: the tensor structure function b₁, the double-helicity-flip ("exotic glue") structure function Δ, the polarized EMC effect, and spectator-tagged DIS on the embedded deuteron.

1 · Why polarized lithium, and why at a collider

The EIC project scope provides polarized electrons, protons and ³He; polarized deuterons are planned as a later addition, and no heavier polarized ion appears in the conceptual design or the Yellow Report [29,25]. The EPIOS white paper [1] makes the case for polarized ⁶Li and ⁷Li — "a desirable addition", listed with ²H and ³He as critical technology — and the ANL source development [51] specifies the beam: an atomic-beam source with optical pumping at 671 nm feeding EBIS charge breeding, at source targets Pz ≥ 0.90 (vector) and Pzz ≥ 0.80 (tensor) and ≥ 10¹³ atoms/s. Four measurements motivate it, each addressing a gap in the literature: no b₁ prediction or measurement exists for any nucleus beyond the deuteron; no gluon-transversity projection exists for any EIC target; no study of α-tagged DIS on lithium exists; and no lithium-beam luminosity or polarization parameter set has been published. The program's simulations convert these gaps into figures of merit.

1.1 · Why lithium as a source: an alkali atom with the right nuclei

Lithium is the lightest alkali metal and its atom is hydrogen-like: a single 2s valence electron whose D lines near 671 nm (670.96 and 670.98 nm in vacuum for ⁷Li) lie within the range of commercial single-frequency diode lasers, and whose ground-state hyperfine splittings, 228.2 MHz for ⁶Li and 803.5 MHz for ⁷Li [35], are small enough for one laser with an electro-optic sideband to address both hyperfine levels. Circularly polarized light pumps the atoms into a single magnetic substate, the hyperfine coupling transfers the electron polarization to the nucleus, and radio-frequency transitions between Zeeman sublevels set any combination of Pz and Pzz [51]. Boron, the other light candidate with a large nuclear spin, "presents a challenge for optical pumping" [1]. The low ionization energy of lithium (5.39 eV [35]) eases ionization, and fully stripped Li³⁺ is within reach of the EBIS being commissioned to charge-breed the polarized ³He⁺⁺ beam, whose design goal is 2×10¹¹ ions per pulse at ≥ 70% polarization [34,1].

The nuclei are the other half of the argument. Both isotopes are stable and abundant (7.6% ⁶Li and 92.4% ⁷Li in natural lithium), and they are the only stable isotopes of one element with J = 1 and J = 3/2: the stable spin-1 nuclei are exactly ²H, ⁶Li and ¹⁴N, the stable spin-3/2 nuclei below A = 30 are ⁷Li, ⁹Be, ¹¹B, ²¹Ne and ²³Na [36]. One source, one laser system and one injector therefore serve a spin-1 and a spin-3/2 program. The EPIOS list of where an exotic-glue discovery is most likely — "spin-1 ⁶Li or ¹⁴N, or spin-3/2 ⁷Li or ²³Na" [1] — reduces, once optical pumping is required, to lithium and sodium, and lithium is the lighter, cluster-structured and ab initio-computable one (§1.2).

The source physics is established rather than speculative: polarized lithium beams have a forty-year history at low energy. The Heidelberg tandem delivered ⁶Li³⁺ at vector polarization 0.57–0.65 in 1977 [30], and by 1987 its atomic-beam source reached Pz, Pzz ≥ 0.85 and accelerated ⁷Li at an alignment Pzz = 0.69 on target [31]; the optically pumped Florida State source delivered more than 150 nA of ⁶Li³⁺ at Pyy = −1.33 ± 0.02 with no polarization loss in the linac [32]; and polarized ⁶Li, ⁷Li and ²³Na beams served two decades of nuclear-reaction studies [33]. New for the EIC are scale and transport: ≥ 10¹³ atoms/s, two to three orders of magnitude beyond these sources, and preservation of the polarization through charge breeding and acceleration beyond 100 GeV/u [51,1].

In the ring, ⁶Li is the easy case. Its gyromagnetic anomaly, G ≈ −0.18, is as small as the deuteron's (−0.14), so it crosses only 81 depolarizing resonances on the way to top energy, against 63 for the deuteron and 2457 for ³He [28]; like the deuteron it is handled with a partial solenoid snake and tune jumps, full Siberian snakes being impractically long for so small a G [28,1], so the deuteron techniques [52] carry over. ⁷Li (G ≈ +1.52) crosses 573 resonances and needs partial snakes with jump quadrupoles [1,28]. The ring rigidity fixes the top energies at 275 GeV × Z/A: 137.5 GeV/u for ⁶Li and 117.9 GeV/u for ⁷Li. Because ⁶Li has A/Z = 2 it runs at the same per-nucleon energy and magnet settings as the deuteron, so e+d and e+⁶Li can be compared at identical kinematics.

1.2 · Why lithium as a target: the spin sits on a light cluster

The two stable lithium isotopes are the lightest nuclei beyond A = 3 in which a polarized light cluster is bound to an inert core: in ⁶Li an α particle (J = 0, T = 0) and a deuteron-like cluster bound by 1.47 MeV against α+d breakup, in ⁷Li an α and a triton bound by 2.47 MeV [36]. Variational Monte Carlo with realistic two- and three-nucleon forces makes this quantitative: the α–d overlap of the ⁶Li ground state integrates to Nαd = 0.86 (0.846 S-wave, 0.017 D-wave), the α–t overlap of ⁷Li to Nαt = 1.00 [17], and the no-core shell model with continuum gives an asymptotic D/S ratio of −0.027 for the d+α configuration [38]. In a fully polarized ⁶Li the α contributes nothing and the two valence nucleons carry the spin: six-body VMC gives valence numbers 1.924 up and 1.076 down, a polarization of 0.85 [17,19]; three-body α+n+p models, in which α+d accounts for 60–70% of the wave function, give above 90% [18]; and E155, which used ⁶LiD as its deuteron target, adopted 87% for the effective deuteron polarization [43]. ⁶Li is therefore a polarized deuteron in the field of an α particle, whose tensor observables can be set against the free deuteron's to isolate the medium effect. The only other stable spin-1 ground state is ¹⁴N, whose spin is shared among p-shell nucleons, with no comparable cluster simplicity and beyond the reach of exact quantum Monte Carlo (A ≤ 12) [40].

In ⁷Li the spin is carried by the unpaired proton of the triton cluster, the neutrons being paired [39]. VMC gives proton numbers 1.934 up and 1.066 down and neutron numbers 1.981 and 2.019 [17], i.e. Pp = 0.87 and Pn = −0.04 (0.866 and −0.037 in the JLab proposal [45]); the shell model already gives Pp = 13/15, and the microscopic calculations confirm it and leave the neutrons almost unpolarized [19,14]. Polarized ⁷Li is thus to the proton what polarized ³He is to the neutron, with the property, decisive for the polarized EMC effect, that the proton sits in a nucleus of normal density. Cloët, Bentz and Thomas set the selection criteria — |Pn| ≪ |Pp| and light, because g₁ᴬ is carried by the valence nucleons and suppressed relative to F₂ᴬ by roughly 1/A — and their candidates are ¹¹B, ¹⁵N, ²⁷Al and ⁷Li, "where the nuclear polarization is largely dominated by the valence proton" [14]; the gluon-spin study names ⁷Li "the most promising case for experimental investigation" [16].

Two further properties matter. The cluster structure makes the nuclei computable: Green's-function Monte Carlo with AV18+IL7 reproduces both binding energies (−31.93 vs −31.99 MeV for ⁶Li, −39.15 vs −39.25 MeV for ⁷Li), the charge radii to within 3%, and, with two-body currents, the magnetic moments (0.835 vs 0.822 μN; 3.24 vs 3.256 μN) [40,39], so the nuclear corrections entering every extraction — effective polarizations, momentum distributions, spectral functions — carry controlled uncertainties rather than model spread. The quadrupole moment of ⁶Li is the exception: the measured Q = −0.0806 fm², against +0.286 fm² for the free deuteron [36], arises from a delicate cancellation between the deuteron's own moment and the D-wave part of the α–d relative motion [19,37], and theory reproduces only its smallness. That smallness is an asset — ⁶Li is nearly spherical, which bounds the deformation term of the coherent channel (§4.2) — while ⁷Li, whose p-shell triton orbit gives Q = −4.00 fm² [36], is the deformed counterpart. The isotopes also share one source, one injector and one detector: the α+d versus α+t comparison changes only the cluster.

1.3 · Why polarization is indispensable

An unpolarized lithium beam would be one more light ion: its inclusive cross section is carried by F₁ and F₂ alone — for an unpolarized target the spin-1 cross section has exactly the spin-½ form [2] — and it would add an A = 6, 7 point to the EMC and shadowing curves that ⁴He and ¹²C already map. Every item of the program is instead a spin observable, and the two flagship items need the tensor moment specifically: b₁ is defined as the difference between the m = 0 and m = ±1 quark distributions, so averaging over m erases it, and Δ needs both a target spin of at least 1 and a transverse alignment axis to define an azimuth (§3). The polarized EMC effect is a statement about g₁, and tagged DIS reads the spin of the cluster (§4). Polarized lithium would be the first polarized ion at a collider with a nuclear core — the first place where the spin structure of a nucleus, rather than of a nucleon, is studied at collider kinematics — and tensor polarization, on which both b₁ and Δ depend, is the scarcer resource.

1.4 · Fixed target or collider?

Polarized lithium has existed for decades as a fixed target, but only in the solid state: dynamically polarized ⁶LiD was the deuteron target of SLAC E155 and of COMPASS, ⁷LiH has been polarized at Saclay (47% for ⁷Li, 56% for the protons) [44], and ⁷LiD (with ⁶LiH or NH₃ as the proton reference) is the material of the approved CLAS12 polarized-EMC experiment [43,42,45]. In these compounds the lithium polarizes along with the hydrogen isotope because all spin species share one spin temperature: COMPASS reached deuteron polarizations of +54%/−47%, whence 51 ± 3% for ⁶Li and 92 ± 4% for the ⁷Li admixture [42]; E155 measured ⁶Li at 97% of the deuteron's [43]; in ⁷LiD at 5 T the CLAS12 proposal expects 65–80% for ⁷Li [45]. Three limits are structural. (i) Dilution: only f ≈ 0.35–0.45 of the nucleons in ⁶LiD are polarizable once the helium coolant is counted — better than 0.22 for ND₃ and 0.15 for NH₃, far from the 1.0 of a stored beam [42,43]. (ii) Tensor polarization: in thermal equilibrium it follows from the vector one, Pzz = 2 − √(4 − 3Pz²), so Pz = 0.5 gives Pzz = 0.20; radio-frequency "hole burning" reaches 29–37% in deuterated butanol at the cost of the vector polarization, and the JLab b₁ experiment is conditionally approved on demonstrating 30% [8,46]. An atomic-beam source prepares the tensor moment directly: HERMES ran its gas target at Pzz = +0.89 and −1.65, "a combination which is not possible for solid-state polarized targets" [5]. (iii) Spectator loss: a spectator α of 100–300 MeV/c carries only 1.3–12 MeV in the target rest frame and stops within 10–250 μm of lithium deuteride [49], inside cells 2–5 cm long at JLab and 30–60 cm at COMPASS, and a coherent ⁶Li recoil at |t| = 0.05 GeV² has only 4.5 MeV. The detectors that do tag slow spectators, BoNuS (protons from 70 MeV/c) and ALERT (α from 230 MeV/c), work with unpolarized gas at a few atmospheres [47,48]; polarized lithium outside dynamically polarized solids is essentially unknown (a 1975 target for a low-energy reaction study [50] is the one instance on record) and has never been used in lepton scattering.

The collider removes each limit at once. The beam is pure lithium, its polarization prepared atom by atom and measured in the ring; the spectator α or triton carries the beam momentum per nucleon into the Roman Pots, off-momentum detectors, B0 and zero-degree calorimeter at a few milliradians, with ~10% pT resolution at 100 MeV/c [22]; the intact coherent recoil can be tagged down to pT ≈ 0 at the IR-8 secondary focus [27]; and √sNN up to 99 GeV for e(18 GeV) × ⁶Li(137.5 GeV/u) opens x down to ~10⁻⁴ at Q² ≥ 1 GeV², where JLab at 11 GeV stops at x ≈ 0.06 and HERMES stopped at 0.01 [45,5] — the region where gluons dominate, where HERMES saw its large b₁, and where coherence holds. What stays with the fixed target is luminosity: 10³⁵ cm⁻² s⁻¹ (CLAS12 at 10–15 nA, Hall C at 115 nA, the material annealed every 2–3 days) [45,8] against an electron–nucleon luminosity of 10³³–10³⁴ cm⁻² s⁻¹ at the EIC [29,25]. The high-x polarized EMC effect and the high-x b₁ of the deuteron are therefore rightly JLab measurements; the EIC takes the low-x and high-Q² regions, the tensor observables at full polarization, and everything that needs a detected spectator or recoil.

What an unpolarized lithium beam already gives. Three things, none of them the driver. Nuclear parton distributions at A = 6 and 7: the high-x EMC ratios of ⁶Li and ⁷Li are in the target list of JLab E12-10-008 [54], motivated by the E03-103 finding that the ⁹Be EMC effect follows the local density of its α clusters rather than its average density [53] — a picture predicting an effect for ⁶Li and ⁷Li well below the other models [54] — with the EIC adding the low-x shadowing region. Cluster virtuality: the α-tagged F₂ of the triton (⁷Li) or deuteron (⁶Li) cluster versus spectator momentum measures the α–t and α–d momentum distributions VMC predicts (Nαt = 1.00 in a relative p-wave, Nαd = 0.86) [17], the collider counterpart of the JLab tagged-EMC program [48]. And coherent diffraction on an α-clustered nucleus, whose t-distribution images the gluon density of the cluster, as studied for ⁷Li at IR-8 [27]. Polarization turns the same structure into a tool: b₁, Δ, g₁ᴬ and the tagged tensor asymmetries all vanish or are undefined without it.

3 · Spin-1 targets

A spin-½ target contributes two spin structure functions, g₁ and g₂. The principal observable is g₁, the helicity-weighted quark distribution, measured through the double-spin asymmetry A∥ ≈ D·g₁/F₁ (D is a kinematic depolarization factor) with both beams longitudinally polarized and the electron helicity reversed bunch by bunch.

Pz is the familiar vector polarization. Pzz, the tensor or alignment polarization, is specific to spin ≥ 1: it distinguishes spin along ±z from spin in the transverse plane (m = 0) and is nonzero even when equal numbers point up and down. An unpolarized beam has (⅓, ⅓, ⅓) and both moments vanish; a pure m = 0 beam has Pz = 0 but Pzz = −2.

Hoodbhoy, Jaffe, and Manohar showed [2] that a spin-1 target has, beyond F₁, F₂, g₁, g₂, four tensor-polarized structure functions b₁–b₄, and Jaffe and Manohar identified one more, the double-helicity-flip function Δ [3]. At leading twist the tensor sector reduces to b₁ (with b₂ = 2x b₁ as its Callan–Gross partner) and Δ. The weights (1, −2, 1) organize all tensor observables: the m = ±1 states enter together and against m = 0, precisely the combination measured by Pzz, and averaging over a fill replaces every cm by Pzz. Two handles follow: with the spin axis longitudinal, the rate difference between fills gives Azz ∝ b₁; with it transverse (θm = 90°), the φ distribution of the scattered electron acquires a cos 2φ modulation proportional to Δ. Both require tensor polarization; neither requires polarized electrons.

⁷Li, with spin 3/2, has four populations and three moments (vector, tensor/quadrupole, octupole). Its rank-0 and rank-1 sectors are exact analogs of the spin-½ case and host the polarized EMC measurement; its rank-2 sector is the formal analog of the spin-1 tensor sector, for which no structure-function basis has been published.

4 · The measurement program

4.1 · The tensor structure function b₁

In parton language, b₁ compares quark distributions in an aligned versus a transverse nucleus [2]:

where q(m) is the quark distribution in a target with spin projection m. For free spin-½ nucleons, or a pure S-wave bound state, all m states are equivalent and b₁ = 0, so a nonzero b₁ measures directly how binding modifies the partonic content: D-wave admixture, pion exchange, hidden-color components, or a tensor-polarized sea (which would violate the Close–Kumano sum rule [4]).

The only existing measurement is HERMES on the deuteron [5]: Azz up to 2×10⁻² over 0.01 < x < 0.45 and b₁(x = 0.012) = 0.112 ± 0.055 ± 0.028, unexpectedly large at low x, with a zero crossing near x ≈ 0.2 and a sum-rule integral 2σ from zero. Convolution calculations predict |b₁| < 10⁻³ at x ≳ 0.2, an order of magnitude below the data [6], whereas a model with pions and a hidden-color probability of only 0.15% reproduces it [7]. JLab E12-13-011 will remeasure 0.16 < x < 0.49 with a solid ND₃ target [8]; the EIC adds the low-x region and the Q² lever arm.

Measurement procedure. The beam is delivered in fills of different tensor polarization ("tensor thirds"); with the estimator (N₊ + N₋ − 2N₀) over matched luminosities, all m-independent physics cancels (§5). At collider kinematics a clean extraction favours orienting the spin axis along the momentum transfer q rather than along the beam [26].

4.2 · Δ(x,Q²): exotic glue

The cos 2φ term corresponds to the virtual photon flipping its helicity by two units between absorption and emission in the forward Compton amplitude. A spin-½ particle cannot absorb two units of helicity, so neither quarks nor bound nucleons generate Δ at leading twist; gluons can. Δ is what the gluon transversity distribution — an interference of gluon helicity states existing only for J ≥ 1 — generates at order αs through a photon–gluon convolution [3,9], so a nonzero Δ in a nucleus is direct evidence for glue not inherited from individual nucleons, hence "exotic glue". It has never been measured; the one prior experimental document is a JLab letter of intent [13].

The measurement uses unpolarized electrons on a tensor-polarized nucleus whose alignment axis lies in the transverse plane, and fits the azimuthal distribution about that axis. Averaged over the fill, the amplitude and its inversion back to Δ are

Taking R from the published R1998 fit [55] rather than a simplified form moves Δ/F₁ by +16.6 / +18.0 / +4.7 / −4.4% at the four sweet spots and roughly halves the luminosity the fast simulation needs for 5σ at Δ/F₁ = 10⁻³: 67.5 / 65.8 / 155.1 fb⁻¹/u at the low, mid and top beam configurations, against 135.3 / 131.3 / 274.6 with the simplified form.

The expected size spans per-mille to percent, and the only absolute normalizations available are moment estimates: a bag-model calculation by Sather and Schmidt for the spin-3/2 Δ⁺⁺ baryon, ∫x Δ dx = −0.012 αs [9], and lattice-QCD calculations by Detmold and Shanahan of gluon transversity in the φ meson (clearly nonzero, at an unphysical pion mass) [10], with NPLQCD finding the analogous matrix element roughly ten times suppressed in the deuteron [11]. Percent-level asymmetries appear only in proton–deuteron Drell–Yan under the maximal assumption ΔTg = Δg, which its authors call optimistic [12]. No calculation exists for ⁶Li or any A > 2 nucleus, and the literature brackets the nuclear Δ both ways: suppressed, as in the lattice deuteron, or enhanced by binding in analogy with the EMC effect [1]. The projection report transfers the bag moment to ⁶Li as a labelled scenario; one EIC year measures the amplitude at ≥ 20σ per bin.

The coherent channel. The same modulation can be measured in coherent diffraction, e ⁶Li → e′ X ⁶Li(g.s.), where the nucleus stays in its ground state and recoils intact. There the modulation mixes two mechanisms: the nuclear shape, a deformed gluon density modulating the diffractive slope (computed for polarized deuterons in the only existing calculation of this kind [23]), and exotic glue proper, which persists at small x where it connects to the elliptic-gluon program [24]. ⁶Li discriminates between them because its quadrupole moment is anomalously small (Q = −0.0806 fm², roughly 50 times smaller than ⁷Li's): the shape term is bounded and linear in t, so a flat-in-t modulation cannot be a shape effect. Detecting the intact recoil is itself nontrivial: the nucleus keeps the beam rigidity and emerges within ~2 mrad of the beam, visible only in the Roman-Pot transverse-momentum tail at IP6 or at the secondary focus of a second interaction region [25,27]. The pot aperture for an intact ⁶Li, measured by tracking one through the ePIC far-forward geometry, opens horizontally — |θx| ≳ 2.0 / 1.35 / 1.03 mrad in the 5×41 / 10×100 / 18×275 optics against |θy| ≳ 1.8–3 mrad — because the beamline images an interaction-point angle onto the pot plane with a horizontal lever of 30.6 m against a vertical few metres (one event per scan point, 30° azimuthal steps, no beam envelope, September-2024 epic-main geometry). That aspect is the opposite of the slot the chain assumed, so the acceptance-induced ⟨cos 2β⟩ changes sign, +0.426 → −0.772, and only the low-energy configuration keeps accepted recoils.

4.3 · The polarized EMC effect: g₁ of a bound proton

The EMC effect — the ~10–20% suppression of F₂ per nucleon in a nucleus at 0.3 ≲ x ≲ 0.7 — has been measured for forty years without a settled explanation. Its spin analog has never been measured at all: does the spin structure of a bound nucleon change more, less, or the same as its momentum structure? The observable is the polarized EMC ratio

where Pp, Pn are the effective nucleon polarizations from nuclear structure. ⁷Li is well suited: Pp = +0.87 against Pn = −0.04 (§1.2) [17,45], so it is to a good approximation one polarized proton plus six spectators, with computable corrections. Two classes of model differ sharply: Cloët–Bentz–Thomas (mean-field, NJL) obtain a polarized EMC effect about twice the unpolarized one [14], Tronchin–Matevosyan–Thomas equal effects [15]. A measurement at the 5% level discriminates between them, and the two map onto the competing mean-field and short-range-correlation pictures of the EMC effect itself. Unique to the EIC is the Q² evolution of g₁, driven by the polarized gluon distribution; the predicted gluon-spin EMC effect is larger still, with ⁷Li the most promising case [16], and the collider measures it free of target-fragment absorption.

Both beams are polarized longitudinally and the electron helicity flips bunch by bunch; the error scales as 1/(PePz√N), so the measurement uses the vector polarization the source delivers most readily.

4.4 · Spectator-tagged DIS

The cluster description of ⁶Li is quantitative (§1.2): the α is inert and the deuteron-like pair carries the nuclear spin at a polarization of 0.85–0.9 in six- and three-body calculations [17,18,19], the program using E155's 0.87 [43]. Detecting the α spectator, which carries beam velocity into the far-forward spectrometers, identifies the struck object as the deuteron cluster, so the ⁶Li beam acts as a source of polarized deuterons at controlled virtuality. The framework is spectator tagging (Cosyn–Weiss [20]): the spectator momentum is measured and extrapolated in its transverse component to the free-cluster pole, recovering free-particle structure with binding effects removed by construction rather than modelled. Tagged tensor asymmetries reach O(1) at spectator momenta of ~300 MeV/c, where the D-wave dominates [41], far larger than the inclusive asymmetries. The benchmark is the e+³He double-tagging study of Friščić et al. [21], where tagging improved on effective-polarization unfolding by a factor 2–3, and 10 at low x.

Far-forward routing determines where each isotope's tag fragment is detected, and it follows from magnetic rigidity alone: a beam-velocity fragment bends in the hadron optics according to the ratio of mass-to-charge ratios R = (m/Z)frag/(m/Z)beam, of which (A/Z)frag/(A/Z)beam is the A·Mu approximation — good to 5×10⁻³, but the α is more bound per nucleon than the lithium it leaves, so it sits below beam rigidity rather than exactly on it.

5 · Rates and statistical precision

All projections use luminosity per nucleon: 10 fb⁻¹/u — 30 weeks at 10³³ cm⁻² s⁻¹ with 60% efficiency in the Yellow Report's accounting [25], "one EIC year" — and 100 fb⁻¹/u for the ten-year program, at the polarizations of §1.5. The statistical uncertainties behind every money plot are

The factor √2 in the tensor uncertainties follows from ⟨cos²2φ⟩ = ½ (equivalently, from the three-fill estimator variance); the polarization sits in the denominator because the fill dilutes the physical asymmetry. These formulas, and their behaviour under realistic φ-acceptance holes (where a binned fit replaces the naive moment), are validated end to end by polligen pseudo-experiments. Tensor observables have a structural advantage when the m = ±1-rich and m = 0-rich states are measured with the same detector and compared bin by bin: in their spin-state ratio the φ acceptance cancels and relative-luminosity errors enter only as a constant, where a single-state fit instead measures the detector's own cos 2φ acceptance harmonic divided by Pzz; and the two-state pattern P₊ = +0.6, P₀ = −1.2 is 1.5× more precise than a single state at the same source purity. The cancellation requires bunch-by-bunch alternation: a 10⁻³ difference of the acceptance harmonic between the states fakes half the signal (reconstruction-chain report §3). The polarimetry scale and the tensor radiative corrections (§1.5) do not cancel.
