Deep inelastic scattering on polarized lithium: the physics behind the planned measurements

2026-08-17 · companion to the cos 2φ projection report and to plans/01 (physics case) · written for students and colleagues joining the program; external numbers carry over from the verified physics-case survey

Polarized lithium beams would provide the EIC with spin-polarized nuclei that are light enough for ab initio computation yet complex enough to exhibit genuine nuclear structure. ⁶Li is a spin-1 nucleus well described as a deuteron bound to an inert α core; ⁷Li is its spin-3/2 partner, an α plus a triton. This note develops, starting from the DIS formalism, the four measurements around which the program is built: the tensor structure function b₁, the double-helicity-flip ("exotic glue") structure function Δ, the polarized EMC effect, and spectator-tagged DIS on the embedded deuteron.

1 · Polarized lithium at the EIC

The EIC baseline includes polarized electrons, protons, deuterons, and ³He. The EPIOS white paper [1] presents the accelerator case for adding polarized ⁶Li and ⁷Li: an atomic-beam source with optical pumping at 671 nm feeding EBIS charge breeding, with source targets Pz ≥ 0.90 (vector) and Pzz ≥ 0.80 (tensor) at ≥ 10¹³ atoms/s. In the ring, ⁶Li is spin-dynamically similar to the deuteron: its small gyromagnetic anomaly (G = −0.178) implies only 81 linear depolarizing resonances up to the top energy of ~138 GeV/u, compared with 2457 for ³He, so the machine techniques developed for deuterons carry over. ⁷Li (G = +1.532) reaches ~117 GeV/u with partial snakes and tune jumps [1].

Four measurements motivate the beam, each addressing a verified gap in the literature: no b₁ prediction or measurement exists for any nucleus beyond the deuteron; no gluon-transversity projection exists for any EIC target; no study of α-tagged DIS on lithium exists; and no lithium-beam luminosity or polarization parameter set has been published. The purpose of the program's simulations is to convert these gaps into figures of merit.

3 · Spin-1 targets

A spin-½ target contributes two spin structure functions, g₁ and g₂. The principal observable is g₁, the helicity-weighted quark distribution, measured through the double-spin asymmetry A∥ ≈ D·g₁/F₁ (D is a kinematic depolarization factor) with both beams longitudinally polarized and the electron helicity reversed bunch by bunch.

A spin-1 target carries additional structure. Its spin state along a chosen axis is specified by three populations p₊, p₀, p₋ of the projections m = +1, 0, −1, and two moments characterize the fill:

Pz is the familiar vector polarization. Pzz, the tensor or alignment polarization, is specific to spin ≥ 1: it distinguishes spin along ±z from spin in the transverse plane (m = 0), and it is nonzero even when equal numbers point up and down. An unpolarized beam has populations (⅓, ⅓, ⅓) and both moments vanish; a pure m = 0 beam has Pz = 0 but Pzz = −2.

Hoodbhoy, Jaffe, and Manohar showed [2] that a spin-1 target has, beyond F₁, F₂, g₁, g₂, four tensor-polarized structure functions b₁–b₄, and Jaffe and Manohar identified one more, the double-helicity-flip function Δ [3]. At leading twist the tensor sector reduces to b₁ (with b₂ = 2x b₁ as its Callan–Gross partner) and Δ. Both require tensor polarization; neither requires polarized electrons.

⁷Li, with spin 3/2, has four populations and three moments (vector, tensor/quadrupole, and octupole). Its rank-0 and rank-1 sectors are exact analogs of the spin-½ case, and host the polarized EMC measurement; its rank-2 sector is the formal analog of the spin-1 tensor sector, for which no structure-function basis has been published — an open theory item the program tracks.

4 · The measurement program

4.1 · The tensor structure function b₁

In parton language, b₁ compares quark distributions in an aligned versus a transverse nucleus [2]:

where q(m) is the quark distribution in a target with spin projection m. For a collection of free spin-½ nucleons, or for a pure S-wave bound state, all m states are equivalent and b₁ = 0. A nonzero b₁ therefore measures directly how binding modifies the partonic content: D-wave admixture, pion exchange, hidden-color components, or a tensor-polarized sea (which would violate the Close–Kumano sum rule [4]).

The only existing measurement is HERMES on the deuteron [5]: Azz up to 2×10⁻² over 0.01 < x < 0.45 and b₁(x = 0.012) = 0.112 ± 0.055 ± 0.028, unexpectedly large at low x, with a zero crossing near x ≈ 0.2 and a sum-rule integral 2σ from zero. Standard convolution calculations predict |b₁| < 10⁻³ at x ≳ 0.2, an order of magnitude below the data [6], whereas a model with pions and a hidden-color probability of only 0.15% reproduces it [7]. The tension is unresolved; JLab E12-13-011 will remeasure the high-x region, and the EIC adds the low-x region, where HERMES observed the large signal, together with the Q² lever arm.

Measurement procedure. The beam is delivered in fills of different tensor polarization ("tensor thirds"). With the estimator (N₊ + N₋ − 2N₀) over matched luminosities, all m-independent physics cancels, and the statistical uncertainty on Azz is √(2/N)/Pzz. ⁶Li would provide the first b₁ of any A > 2 nucleus, for which no prediction exists, and the difference between ⁶Li and the free deuteron isolates the effect of embedding in the α field. One systematic is tracked from the outset: at collider kinematics a clean b₁ extraction favors orienting the spin axis along the momentum transfer q rather than along the beam [26].

4.2 · Δ(x,Q²): exotic glue

The cos 2φ term in the master formula corresponds to the virtual photon flipping its helicity by two units between absorption and emission in the forward Compton amplitude. A spin-½ particle cannot absorb two units of helicity, so neither quarks nor bound nucleons generate Δ at leading twist; gluons can. Δ is the structure function that the gluon transversity distribution, an interference of gluon helicity states existing only in targets with J ≥ 1, generates at order αs through a photon–gluon convolution [3,9]. A nonzero Δ in a nucleus is therefore direct evidence for glue not inherited from individual nucleons, hence the name "exotic glue". It has not been measured for any target; the one prior experimental document is a JLab letter of intent [13].

The expected size spans per-mille to percent. The only absolute normalizations available are moment estimates: a bag-model calculation by Sather and Schmidt for the spin-3/2 Δ⁺⁺ baryon, ∫x Δ dx = −0.012 αs [9], and lattice-QCD calculations by Detmold and Shanahan of gluon transversity in the φ meson (clearly nonzero, albeit at an unphysical pion mass) [10], with NPLQCD finding the analogous matrix element roughly ten times suppressed in the deuteron [11]. Percent-level asymmetries appear only in proton–deuteron Drell–Yan under the maximal assumption ΔTg = Δg, described by its authors as optimistic [12]. No calculation exists for ⁶Li or any A > 2 nucleus, and the literature brackets the nuclear Δ in both directions: suppressed, as in the lattice deuteron, or enhanced by binding in analogy with the EMC effect [1]. The projection report transfers the bag moment to ⁶Li as a labeled scenario, shows that one EIC year measures the amplitude at ≥ 20σ per bin, and details the caveats.

The coherent channel. The same modulation can be measured in coherent diffraction, e ⁶Li → e′ X ⁶Li(g.s.), in which the nucleus remains in its ground state and recoils intact. There the cos 2φ modulation mixes two mechanisms: the nuclear shape, in which a deformed gluon density modulates the diffractive slope (the mechanism computed for polarized deuterons in the only existing calculation of this kind [23]), and exotic glue proper. ⁶Li discriminates between the two because its quadrupole moment is anomalously small (Q = −0.0806 fm², roughly 50 times smaller than that of ⁷Li): the shape term is bounded and linear in t, so a flat-in-t modulation cannot be a shape effect. Detection of the intact recoil is itself nontrivial: the nucleus retains the beam rigidity exactly (A/Z = 2) and emerges within ~2 mrad of the beam, visible only in the Roman-Pot transverse-momentum tail at IP6 or at the secondary focus of a second interaction region [25,27]; the companion report treats this in detail.

4.3 · The polarized EMC effect: g₁ of a bound proton

The EMC effect — the ~10–20% suppression of F₂ per nucleon in a nucleus at 0.3 ≲ x ≲ 0.7 — has been measured for forty years without a settled explanation. Its spin analog has never been measured at all: does the spin structure of a bound nucleon change more, less, or the same as its momentum structure? The observable is the polarized EMC ratio

where Pp, Pn are the effective nucleon polarizations from nuclear structure. ⁷Li is well suited: quantum Monte Carlo gives Pp = +0.866 and Pn = −0.037 [17], so to a good approximation polarized ⁷Li is one polarized proton plus six spectators, with computable corrections. Two classes of models make sharply different predictions: Cloët–Bentz–Thomas (mean-field, NJL) obtain a polarized EMC effect about twice the unpolarized one [14], whereas Tronchin–Matevosyan–Thomas obtain equal effects [15]. A measurement at the 5% level discriminates between them, and the two map onto the competing mean-field and short-range-correlation pictures of the EMC effect itself. A handle unique to the EIC is the Q² evolution of g₁, driven by the polarized gluon distribution; the predicted gluon-spin EMC effect is larger still, with ⁷Li identified as the most promising case [16]. JLab E12-14-001 will measure ⁷Li at x > 0.06; the EIC extends the coverage to x < 0.06 and adds the Q² arm, in collider kinematics free of target-fragment absorption.

For this observable both beams are polarized longitudinally and the electron helicity flips bunch-by-bunch; the error scales as 1/(PePz√N), so it uses the vector polarization the source delivers most readily.

4.4 · Spectator-tagged DIS

The cluster description of ⁶Li is quantitative: cluster-model calculations give the deuteron-like pair a polarization of ≈0.87 within ⁶Li, carrying over 90% of the nuclear spin [18]. When the α spectator is detected (it carries beam velocity into the far-forward spectrometers), the struck object is identified as the deuteron cluster, and the ⁶Li beam acts as a source of polarized deuterons at controlled virtuality. The formal framework is spectator tagging (Cosyn–Weiss [20]): the spectator momentum is measured and extrapolated in its transverse component to the free-nucleon (here free-cluster) pole, recovering free-particle structure with binding effects removed by construction rather than modeled. Tagged tensor asymmetries are predicted to reach O(1) at spectator momenta of ~300 MeV/c, where the D-wave dominates — far larger than the inclusive asymmetries above. The benchmark for the method is the e+³He double-tagging study of Friščić et al. [21], in which tagging improved on effective-polarization unfolding by a factor of 2–3, and by a factor of 10 at low x.

Far-forward routing determines where each isotope's tag fragment is detected, and it follows from magnetic rigidity alone: a beam-velocity fragment with mass-to-charge ratio A/Z bends in the hadron optics according to R = (A/Z)frag/(A/Z)beam.

These formulas, and their behavior under realistic φ-acceptance holes (where a binned fit replaces the naive moment), are validated end to end by pseudo-experiments with the polligen event generator. Tensor observables enjoy a structural advantage in systematics: the cos 2φ modulation is self-normalized within a single fill, so relative-luminosity errors largely cancel; what remains is the polarimetry scale (δPzz/Pzz ≈ 3%) and radiative corrections, which for tensor observables have not yet been calculated.
