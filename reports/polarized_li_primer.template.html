Deep inelastic scattering on polarized lithium: the physics behind the planned measurements

2026-08-17 · companion to the cos 2φ projection report and to plans/01 (physics case) · written for students and colleagues joining the program; external numbers carry over from the fetch-verified physics-case survey

A polarized lithium beam gives the EIC something no other beam provides: spin-polarized nuclei light enough to compute, heavy enough to have genuine nuclear structure. ⁶Li is a spin-1 nucleus that behaves in many ways like a deuteron bound to an inert α core; ⁷Li is its spin-3/2 partner, an α plus a triton. This note explains, from the DIS formalism up, the four measurements the program is built around: the tensor structure function b₁, the double-helicity-flip ("exotic glue") structure function Δ, the polarized EMC effect, and spectator-tagged DIS on the embedded deuteron.

1 · Why lithium, and what the source must deliver

The EIC baseline includes polarized electrons, protons, deuterons, and ³He. The EPIOS white paper [1] lays out the accelerator case for adding polarized ⁶Li and ⁷Li: an atomic-beam source with optical pumping at 671 nm feeding EBIS charge breeding, with source targets Pz ≥ 0.90 (vector) and Pzz ≥ 0.80 (tensor) at ≥ 10¹³ atoms/s. In the ring, ⁶Li is spin-dynamically deuteron-like — its small gyromagnetic anomaly (G = −0.178) means only 81 linear depolarizing resonances up to the top energy of ~138 GeV/u, compared with 2457 for ³He — so the machine techniques developed for deuterons carry over. ⁷Li (G = +1.532) reaches ~117 GeV/u with partial snakes and tune jumps [1].

Four measurements motivate the beam, and each sits on a verified literature gap: no b₁ prediction or measurement exists for any nucleus beyond the deuteron; no gluon-transversity projection exists for any EIC target; no study of α-tagged DIS on lithium exists; and no Li-beam luminosity/polarization parameter set has been published. The program's simulations exist to turn these gaps into figures of merit.

3 · What spin-1 adds

A spin-½ target contributes two spin structure functions, g₁ and g₂. The workhorse is g₁, the helicity-weighted quark distribution, measured through the double-spin asymmetry A∥ ≈ D·g₁/F₁ (D is a kinematic depolarization factor) with both beams longitudinally polarized and the electron helicity flipped bunch by bunch.

A spin-1 target is genuinely richer. Its spin state along a chosen axis is a set of three populations p₊, p₀, p₋ over the projections m = +1, 0, −1, and two numbers characterize the fill:

Pz is the familiar vector polarization. Pzz — the tensor or alignment polarization — is new: it distinguishes "spin along ±z" from "spin in the transverse plane" (m = 0), and it is nonzero even when equal numbers point up and down. An unpolarized beam has (⅓, ⅓, ⅓) and both moments vanish; a pure m = 0 beam has Pz = 0 but Pzz = −2.

Hoodbhoy, Jaffe, and Manohar showed [2] that a spin-1 target has, beyond F₁, F₂, g₁, g₂, four tensor-polarized structure functions b₁–b₄, and Jaffe and Manohar identified one more, the double-helicity-flip function Δ [3]. At leading twist the tensor sector reduces to b₁ (with b₂ = 2x b₁ as its Callan–Gross partner) and Δ. Two experimental handles follow: with the spin axis longitudinal, the rate difference between fills gives Azz ∝ b₁; with the spin axis transverse (θm = 90°), the φ distribution of the scattered electron acquires a cos 2φ modulation proportional to Δ. Both need tensor polarization and neither needs polarized electrons.

⁷Li, with spin 3/2, has four populations and three moments (vector, tensor/quadrupole, octupole). Its rank-0 and rank-1 sectors are exact analogs of the spin-½ case — that is where the polarized EMC measurement lives — and its rank-2 sector is the formal analog of the spin-1 tensor sector, for which no structure-function basis has been published (an open theory item the program tracks).

4 · The four measurements

4.1 · b₁: does a bound deuteron keep its shape?

In parton language, b₁ compares quark distributions in an aligned versus a transverse nucleus [2]:

where q(m) is the quark distribution in a target with spin projection m. For a collection of free spin-½ nucleons, or a pure S-wave bound state, all m states look alike and b₁ = 0. A nonzero b₁ is therefore a direct measure of how binding deforms the nucleon content: D-wave admixture, pion exchange, hidden-color components, or a tensor-polarized sea (which would violate the Close–Kumano sum rule [4]).

The single existing measurement is HERMES on the deuteron [5]: Azz up to 2×10⁻² over 0.01 < x < 0.45, b₁(x = 0.012) = 0.112 ± 0.055 ± 0.028 — surprisingly large at low x, with a zero crossing near x ≈ 0.2 and a sum-rule integral 2σ away from zero. Standard convolution calculations predict |b₁| < 10⁻³ at x ≳ 0.2, an order of magnitude below the data [6]; a model with pions plus only a 0.15% hidden-color probability can reproduce it [7]. The tension is unresolved, and JLab E12-13-011 will remeasure the high-x region. The EIC adds the low-x region where HERMES saw the large signal, and the Q² lever arm.

How the measurement runs. The beam is delivered in fills of different tensor polarization ("tensor thirds"); with the estimator (N₊ + N₋ − 2N₀) over matched luminosities, all m-independent physics cancels and the statistical error on Azz is √(2/N)/Pzz. ⁶Li would be the first b₁ of any A > 2 nucleus — there is not even a prediction to compare to — and the ⁶Li-versus-free-deuteron difference isolates what embedding in the α field does to the bound deuteron. A practical systematic tracked from the start: at collider kinematics the clean b₁ extraction prefers the spin axis along the momentum transfer q rather than along the beam [26].

4.2 · Δ(x,Q²): exotic glue

The cos 2φ term in the master formula has a special pedigree. It corresponds to the virtual photon flipping its helicity by two units between absorption and emission in the forward Compton amplitude. A spin-½ particle cannot absorb two units of angular momentum along the collinear axis — so no quark, and no bound proton or neutron, can generate Δ at leading twist. Gluons can. Δ is the structure function that the gluon transversity distribution — an interference of gluon helicity states existing only in targets with J ≥ 1 — generates at order αs through a photon–gluon convolution [3,9]. Any nonzero Δ in a nucleus is direct evidence for glue not inherited from individual nucleons — hence "exotic glue". It has never been measured for any target; the one prior experimental document is a JLab letter of intent [13].

The expected size spans per-mille to percent. The only absolute normalizations available are moment estimates: a bag-model calculation by Sather and Schmidt for the spin-3/2 Δ⁺⁺ baryon, ∫x Δ dx = −0.012 αs [9], and lattice-QCD calculations by Detmold and Shanahan of gluon transversity in the φ meson — clearly nonzero, at an unphysical pion mass [10] — with NPLQCD finding the analogous matrix element roughly ten times suppressed in the deuteron [11]. Percent-level asymmetries appear only in proton–deuteron Drell–Yan under the maximal assumption ΔTg = Δg, which its authors call optimistic [12]. No calculation exists for ⁶Li or any A > 2 nucleus, and the literature brackets nuclear Δ in both directions — suppressed as in the lattice deuteron, or enhanced by binding like the EMC effect [1]. The projection report ports the bag moment to ⁶Li as a labeled scenario, shows that one EIC year measures the amplitude at ≥ 20σ per bin, and spells out the caveats.

The coherent variant. The same modulation can be measured in coherent diffraction, e ⁶Li → e′ X ⁶Li(g.s.), where the nucleus stays in its ground state and recoils intact. There the cos 2φ modulation mixes two mechanisms: the nuclear shape (a deformed gluon density modulates the diffractive slope — the mechanism computed for polarized deuterons in the only existing calculation of this kind [23]) and exotic glue proper. ⁶Li is a sharp discriminator because its quadrupole moment is anomalously small (Q = −0.0806 fm², about 50× smaller than ⁷Li's): the shape term is bounded and t-linear, so a flat-in-t modulation cannot be a shape effect. Detecting the intact recoil is its own experimental story — the nucleus keeps exactly the beam rigidity (A/Z = 2) and emerges within ~2 mrad of the beam, visible only in the Roman-Pot transverse-momentum tail at IP6, or at the secondary focus of a second interaction region [25,27] — told in the companion report.

4.3 · The polarized EMC effect: g₁ of a bound proton

The EMC effect — the ~10–20% suppression of F₂ per nucleon in a nucleus at 0.3 ≲ x ≲ 0.7 — has been measured for forty years without a settled explanation. Its spin analog has never been measured at all: does the spin structure of a bound nucleon change more, less, or the same as its momentum structure? The observable is the polarized EMC ratio

where Pp, Pn are the effective nucleon polarizations from nuclear structure. ⁷Li is nearly ideal: quantum Monte Carlo gives Pp = +0.866, Pn = −0.037 [17] — to a good approximation, polarized ⁷Li is one polarized proton plus six spectators, with computable corrections. The model stakes are set by two camps: Cloët–Bentz–Thomas (mean-field, NJL) predict a polarized EMC effect about twice the unpolarized one [14]; Tronchin–Matevosyan–Thomas predict them equal [15]. A 5%-level measurement discriminates the two, and they map onto the competing mean-field versus short-range-correlation pictures of the EMC effect itself. A further EIC-only handle: the Q² evolution of g₁ is driven by the polarized gluon distribution, and the predicted gluon-spin EMC effect is larger still, with ⁷Li named the most promising case [16]. JLab E12-14-001 will measure ⁷Li at x > 0.06; the EIC extends to x < 0.06 and adds the Q² arm, in collider kinematics free of target-fragment absorption.

For this observable both beams are polarized longitudinally and the electron helicity flips bunch-by-bunch; the error scales as 1/(PePz√N), so it uses the vector polarization the source delivers most readily.

4.4 · Tagged DIS: operating on the embedded deuteron

⁶Li ≈ α + d is more than an analogy: cluster-model calculations give the deuteron-like pair a polarization of ~0.87 inside ⁶Li, carrying over 90% of the nuclear spin [18]. If the α spectator is detected — it carries beam velocity into the far-forward spectrometers — the event is known to have struck the deuteron cluster, and the ⁶Li beam becomes a source of polarized deuterons at controlled virtuality. The formal machinery is spectator tagging (Cosyn–Weiss [20]): measure the spectator momentum, extrapolate in its transverse momentum to the free-nucleon (here free-cluster) pole, and recover free-particle structure with binding effects removed by construction rather than modeled. Tagged tensor asymmetries are predicted to reach O(1) at spectator momenta ~300 MeV/c, where the D-wave dominates — enormous compared with the inclusive asymmetries above. The benchmark for the method's power is the e+³He double-tagging study of Friščić et al. [21]: tagging beat effective-polarization unfolding by ×2–3, and by ×10 at low x.

Far-forward routing decides which isotope tags where, and it follows from magnetic rigidity alone. A beam-velocity fragment with mass-to-charge A/Z bends in the hadron optics according to R = (A/Z)frag/(A/Z)beam:

These formulas — and their breakdown under realistic φ acceptance holes, where a binned fit replaces the naive moment — are validated end-to-end by the polligen event generator's pseudo-experiments. Tensor observables have a structural advantage on systematics: the cos 2φ modulation is self-normalized within a single fill, so relative-luminosity errors largely cancel; what remains is the polarimetry scale (δPzz/Pzz ≈ 3%) and radiative corrections, which for tensor observables are uncalculated to date.
